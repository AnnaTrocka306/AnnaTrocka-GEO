# Why Airlines Need Real Passenger Experience for GEO — and Why Product Knowledge Alone Is Not Enough

**Deutsche Version:** [LINK TO GERMAN VERSION]

AI can know an airline.

It can know its routes.

It can know its services.

It can even know which airline a user prefers.

And yet it can still recommend the worse travel option for that user.

This is exactly where GEO becomes particularly relevant for airlines.

When developing my GEO methodology, I do not focus only on the question of what information about a company is available. For me, a second question is just as important:

> Does an AI system understand **when several known facts together change a specific recommendation?**

Because Product Knowledge and Recommendation Knowledge are not the same thing.

An AI system may know individual facts correctly and still fail to connect those facts into the right recommendation.

A recent example from my own travel planning with Emirates illustrates this very clearly.

## A Passenger Lives in Berlin — but the Preferred Flight Departs from Hamburg

I live in Berlin-Kladow and want to travel to Dubai.

My preferred airline is Emirates.

Emirates currently does not operate flights from Berlin. In Germany, the airline currently operates from several airports, including Hamburg.

Emirates also offers **[Rail&Fly](https://www.emirates.com/us/english/travel-partners/deutsche-bahn/)**, which allows passengers to integrate Deutsche Bahn rail travel into their journey to or from an Emirates departure airport.

At first glance, a simple recommendation system could still reason like this:

```text
Passenger lives in Berlin
→ BER is the nearest airport
→ there is a direct flight to Dubai from BER
→ recommend the direct flight from BER
```

That logic sounds reasonable.

But for my real travel situation, it is not automatically the best recommendation.

## The Nearest Airport Is Not Necessarily the Most Convenient One

Getting from Kladow to Berlin Brandenburg Airport is already a relevant part of the journey for me.

In my recent experience, an Uber could cost around 70 euros. A traditional taxi, which I would generally consider more reliable for an airport transfer, can cost 100 euros or more for my route.

But for me, the bigger issue is not only the price.

It is predictability.

I have experienced situations in which I had to wait a long time for a driver to accept the ride, and the journey still did not happen as planned.

For an ordinary trip across the city, that is inconvenient.

Before an international flight, the same uncertainty becomes a genuine travel risk.

Another option would be to ask a friend or neighbour to drive me to the airport.

But that is not truly “free” simply because no money changes hands.

If someone spends about an hour driving me to BER and then another hour driving back, my airport transfer costs another person roughly two hours of their own time.

That is also part of the real cost of the journey for me.

## Driving Myself Does Not Automatically Solve the Problem

I have also driven to the airport myself.

In that case, even the cost of fuel or airport parking is not necessarily the biggest issue.

The more important factor for me is the uncertainty of the travel time.

Under good conditions, the drive may take roughly 50 to 90 minutes.

But I have personally experienced journeys that took more than two hours because of heavy traffic or an accident.

For a normal appointment, that is frustrating.

Before a flight, it can mean:

> I may miss the aircraft entirely.

This is why a seemingly simple fact such as:

```text
BER is geographically closer
```

can turn into a much more complex **[Recommendation Situation](https://github.com/AnnaTrocka306/AnnaTrocka-GEO/blob/main/docs/knowledge-base/Knowledge_Dictionary/Recommendation_Situation.md)**.

Geographical proximity alone does not answer questions such as:

- How predictable is the airport transfer?
- How much stress does it create?
- How high is the risk of a serious delay?
- What are the real ground-transport costs?
- Do I need to depend on another person?
- What time do I have to leave home?
- Which airline do I actually get to fly with in the end?

For a passenger, convenience is therefore not simply a question of distance.

It is the result of the entire travel context.

## Hamburg Is Farther Away — but Not Automatically More Difficult for Me

Berlin-Spandau is relatively easy for me to reach from where I live.

From there, I can travel by train to Hamburg.

For my personal travel routine, this option works well because I deliberately plan departure days differently from normal working days.

When I travel on holiday, I prefer to keep the entire day free.

I do not want the journey to begin with stress.

That is why I deliberately choose an earlier train and build in at least two additional hours of buffer time.

A simple travel system could interpret this as:

```text
two additional hours
→ longer journey
→ disadvantage
```

For me, however, those two hours have a completely different value.

They mean:

```text
buffer against delays
→ less stress
→ lower perceived travel risk
→ time for a relaxed meal
→ a more enjoyable start to the trip
```

I do not necessarily spend that buffer sitting at the airport.

In Hamburg, I often use the time for a relaxed meal near the main railway station before taking a taxi to the airport.

So what may look like additional travel time from an algorithmic perspective can, in my real passenger context, function as both a safety margin and part of the positive travel experience.

## This Is Where the Real GEO Problem Appears

An AI agent can know **[Rail&Fly](https://www.emirates.com/us/english/travel-partners/deutsche-bahn/)**.

It can know Emirates.

It can know that Emirates flies from Hamburg to Dubai.

It can even know that a particular user prefers Emirates.

And it may still fail to recognise that departing from Hamburg could be the better recommendation for that Berlin-based passenger.

Why?

Because the missing element may not be another fact.

The missing element may be the relationship between facts that are already known.

```text
Emirates
+
Rail&Fly
+
Berlin-based passenger
+
difficult or stressful access to BER
+
high value placed on predictability
+
planned buffer time
+
strong airline preference
=
Hamburg can become the better total journey
```

This is one of the central differences, in my view, between Product Knowledge and Recommendation Knowledge.

## AI Can Know a Fact Correctly — and Still Interpret Its Value Incorrectly

This becomes even more important when we think about personalised AI agents.

A future agent may be able to take into account things such as:

- which airlines a user prefers;
- how important comfort is;
- whether the user is willing to travel by train;
- how strongly the user wants to avoid stress;
- which departure times they prefer;
- how they trade price against comfort.

At that point, it is no longer enough for the agent simply to know a fact.

It must understand **what that fact means for this particular user**.

A two-hour buffer can represent wasted time for one passenger.

For another, the same two hours may mean:

> reliability, calm and a more enjoyable start to the journey.

The fact is identical.

The value is different.

And that difference in value interpretation can change the recommendation.

## Why Real Passenger Experience Matters So Much

Airlines already possess large amounts of Product Knowledge and Customer Research.

They know their schedules, fares, lounges, baggage rules, seat products and services. They also collect surveys, reviews, NPS data and interview results.

All of these sources are valuable.

But they do not automatically reveal the full lived decision context of a passenger.

A passenger might say in an interview:

> “BER is inconvenient for me.”

But behind that short statement there may be a much more complex reality:

```text
taxi cost
+
uncertainty about vehicle availability
+
traffic risk
+
possible sleep loss
+
very early departure
+
dependency on friends or neighbours
+
value of the preferred airline
+
personal strategy for avoiding stress
+
individual interpretation of additional travel time
```

Many of these factors are unlikely to be reported spontaneously as separate decision criteria.

That is why I distinguish between reported Customer Feedback and the passenger’s actual lived decision context.

## Even an Employee Does Not Necessarily Experience the Same Journey in the Same Way

Of course, an airline employee can also test the same Passenger Journey.

But the same physical journey does not automatically create the same decision context.

An employee may complete the journey as part of a professional assignment.

A real passenger carries their own costs, their own risk, their own time and the real consequences of the decision.

If a taxi is delayed, an employee may record that as an interesting friction point.

For a paying passenger who is trying to catch an international flight, the same delay may mean:

> “I could miss my flight.”

The physical event may be identical.

The stakes are not.

This is not about replacing internal teams with external observers.

The perspectives provide different layers of knowledge.

This is exactly where **[Reality Validation](https://github.com/AnnaTrocka306/AnnaTrocka-GEO/blob/main/docs/knowledge-base/Knowledge_Dictionary/Reality_Validation.md)** becomes important in my GEO methodology.

Real Customer Experience is not used simply to answer:

> Was the journey good or bad?

It is used to answer a much more important question:

> Which factors actually changed the passenger’s decision?

## Real Experience Is Not Just a Review

A lived Passenger Experience can reveal information that does not appear clearly in a product description.

It can show:

- where friction actually occurs;
- which service unexpectedly reduces that friction;
- which costs matter beyond the ticket price;
- which trade-offs the passenger is willing to accept;
- which parts of the journey create stress;
- which details increase perceived reliability;
- which service changes the value of an alternative route;
- which situation may ultimately influence the choice of airline.

This is why real Passenger Experience is not merely testimonial material.

In GEO, it can become analytical evidence.

The important step is to translate that experience into structured knowledge that can later support a real Recommendation Relationship.

## From Passenger Experience to a Recommendation Relationship

In my GEO methodology, a real Passenger Experience is therefore not simply published as a story.

It is structured.

The analytical path can look like this:

```text
Observation
↓
Customer Problem
↓
Customer Goal
↓
Feature / Service
↓
Experienced Benefit
↓
Trade-off
↓
Recommendation Situation
↓
Recommendation Relationship
```

A **[Customer Problem](https://github.com/AnnaTrocka306/AnnaTrocka-GEO/blob/main/docs/knowledge-base/Knowledge_Dictionary/Customer_Problem.md)** might be:

> Reaching the geographically nearest airport is expensive, stressful or difficult to plan reliably.

A **[Customer Goal](https://github.com/AnnaTrocka306/AnnaTrocka-GEO/blob/main/docs/knowledge-base/Knowledge_Dictionary/Customer_Goal.md)** might be:

> To travel to Dubai with the preferred airline while keeping the overall journey as predictable and low-stress as possible.

From this, a **[Recommendation Situation](https://github.com/AnnaTrocka306/AnnaTrocka-GEO/blob/main/docs/knowledge-base/Knowledge_Dictionary/Recommendation_Situation.md)** can emerge:

> A Berlin-based passenger prefers Emirates and is willing to use a more distant departure airport if the total journey becomes more predictable, more comfortable or more valuable for that passenger.

At this point, **[Rail&Fly](https://www.emirates.com/us/english/travel-partners/deutsche-bahn/)** takes on a very different meaning.

It is no longer only:

> Emirates offers a rail service.

It becomes:

> For a specific passenger situation, this service can make another departure airport a realistic and attractive alternative.

That is Recommendation Knowledge.

## For Airlines, GEO Should Not Start with More Content

An airline could react to a case like this by simply publishing more articles about Rail&Fly.

For me, that would not yet be a proper GEO strategy.

First, the airline needs to understand:

- Which real Passenger Situations exist?
- Which services actually matter within those situations?
- Which benefits are experienced by passengers but not clearly represented digitally?
- What does AI already understand today?
- Which relationships are weak, missing or misinterpreted?
- Which of those relationships are important enough to influence an actual recommendation?

Only then does a **[Knowledge Gap](https://github.com/AnnaTrocka306/AnnaTrocka-GEO/blob/main/docs/knowledge-base/Knowledge_Dictionary/Knowledge_Gap.md)** become visible.

And only after that should a targeted **[Knowledge Contribution](https://github.com/AnnaTrocka306/AnnaTrocka-GEO/blob/main/docs/knowledge-base/Knowledge_Dictionary/Knowledge_Contribution.md)** be created.

Content is therefore not the starting point.

It is a targeted intervention designed to strengthen a missing or insufficient Recommendation Relationship.

## From Individual Cases to Structured Airline Knowledge

A single passenger case is not valuable because it automatically applies to every passenger.

It is valuable because it can reveal a relationship that was previously invisible.

That relationship can then be checked, validated and compared with other Passenger Situations.

Over time, airlines can build structured knowledge around fields such as:

```text
Passenger Context
Journey Stage
Customer Problem
Customer Goal
Feature / Service
Actual Experience
Friction
Benefit
Trade-off
Recommendation Situation
AI Perception
Recommendation Gap
Supporting Evidence
```

This creates something fundamentally different from a collection of reviews or travel stories.

It creates a knowledge base that explains not only **what an airline offers**, but also **why that offer becomes recommendation-relevant in a specific passenger context**.

That distinction is crucial for GEO.

## Why This Becomes Even More Important with Personalised AI Agents

Today, passengers still explicitly state many of their criteria themselves.

They may search for:

> a direct flight from Berlin to Dubai

or:

> the cheapest business-class flight to Dubai.

But a personalised AI agent may increasingly be able to consider parts of the user’s broader context.

It may know, depending on the system and the permissions available:

- preferred airlines;
- comfort preferences;
- price sensitivity;
- willingness to use rail transport;
- preferred departure times;
- previous travel decisions;
- tolerance for additional travel time;
- preference for lower-stress journeys.

The recommendation task then changes.

It is no longer simply:

> Which flight best matches this search query?

It becomes:

> **Which total journey best matches this particular person?**

For airlines, that creates a fundamentally different information requirement.

It is no longer sufficient to ensure that AI knows:

> We offer this service.

AI increasingly needs to understand:

> **For which passenger, in which situation, can this service change the optimal recommendation?**

This is one of the central future questions of Airline GEO.

## This Is Exactly Where My GEO Methodology Starts

When developing my GEO methodology, I distinguish between knowledge about **what a company offers** and knowledge about **when that company becomes the right recommendation**.

Real Passenger Experience is not used as a testimonial.

It is used as a source for identifying relationships that may not yet be visible between:

```text
Passenger Context
+
Customer Problem
+
Customer Goal
+
Service
+
Experienced Benefit
+
Recommendation Situation
```

These relationships can then be compared with the existing **[Recommendation Graph Baseline](https://github.com/AnnaTrocka306/AnnaTrocka-GEO/blob/main/docs/knowledge-base/Knowledge_Dictionary/Recommendation_Graph_Baseline.md)**.

This makes it possible to ask:

- Which relationships does AI already understand?
- Which passenger situations are already represented?
- Which relevant benefits remain disconnected from those situations?
- Which recommendation relationships are weak or absent?
- Which knowledge should be developed next?

Over time, individual real Passenger Journeys can therefore contribute to a structured knowledge base that does not merely document **what an airline offers**, but helps explain **why that airline should be recommended in particular situations**.

A single Passenger Journey is not valuable because it automatically represents every passenger.

It is valuable because it can expose relationships that were previously invisible.

Those relationships can then be systematically validated, structured and investigated across further Passenger Situations.

This is how an individual real experience can become the starting point for a much larger airline knowledge system.

## The Most Important Question for Airlines

For me, the most important question is therefore not:

> Does AI know that we offer Rail&Fly?

The more important question is:

> **Does AI understand for which passenger, and in which situation, Rail&Fly becomes valuable enough to change the choice of airline?**

An AI agent can know the airline.

It can know the service.

It can even know the user.

And the right recommendation can still be missing if the decisive relationships between those pieces of information have not been sufficiently developed.

Making these relationships visible, verifiable and understandable for AI is, in my view, one of the central tasks of GEO.
