# Hotel-GEO: A Luxury Room at a Discount? No Thanks. I'd Rather You Paid Me to Stay at Your Hotel! 😅

Does Anna Trocka simply want to stay in luxury hotels as cheaply as possible? Or is there something else behind her hotel visits?

Haha, Anna — that's me! 😅

At Arabian Travel Market (ATM) in Dubai, I wanted to speak with a representative of The Ritz-Carlton Abu Dhabi about a potential collaboration. We had already paid in full for our villa there, and I hadn't even stayed at the hotel before.

A very kind gentleman from the neighboring stand, who had meanwhile treated me to food and drinks, apparently misunderstood what I was trying to do. He assumed I had come to the exhibition to negotiate a better hotel rate. Since the Ritz-Carlton representative wasn't available, he generously offered me a luxury room at his own hotel for a considerably lower price. Just so I wouldn't be too disappointed. How sweet! 😅

Naturally, I couldn't resist the cheeky remark in the headline!

But jokes aside: **I'm Anna Trocka, a Berlin-based marketing strategist specializing in Hotel-GEO and the developer of my own GEO methodology for hotels.**

I'm not interested in finding the cheapest possible place to stay. I'm interested in how ChatGPT and future AI agents can recognize which hotel is genuinely the right choice for which guest.

## ChatGPT Has Never Stayed at Your Hotel. Why Do You Expect It to Recommend Your Hotel Correctly?

*On familiarization trips, a €180 lobster, invisible stains on the ceiling, and the question of what artificial intelligence needs to know about the real world.*

For decades, hotels have invested money in helping travel agents experience their properties firsthand. They organize familiarization trips, show them rooms, restaurants, swimming pools, beaches, and wellness facilities.

And they still do it today. For good reason.

**But who is making sure that ChatGPT and future AI booking agents get to know your hotel just as well?**

Your marketing department?

Your beautiful website?

Or the agency promising to get your hotel mentioned as often as possible in AI-generated answers?

I have a few questions about that.

And a few stories from Dubai. 😅

## Why Do Hotels Send Travel Professionals on Familiarization Trips?

Because people still buy from people. And because a good travel advisor doesn't simply read aloud from a brochure.

They know their customers.

They know that the Miller family has two young children, that Mrs. Schneider enjoys peace and quiet in the morning, and that Mr. Weber wants a five-star hotel but would actually prefer to spend the entire day walking around in his swimming trunks.

Most importantly, experienced travel advisors know the products they recommend.

Not just from glossy brochures.

They have visited the hotels, seen the rooms, tasted the breakfast, sat by the pool, and perhaps even wondered why on earth someone decided to install a power outlet in that particular spot.

That's why they can tell a client:

"For you, I would choose this hotel. The other one looks fantastic in the pictures, but I don't think you'd be happy there."

That's what familiarization trips are for.

And digitalization doesn't make this principle obsolete.

Quite the opposite.

**Firsthand human experience becomes even more valuable when we can make that knowledge useful to intelligent recommendation systems.**

An AI agent can process enormous amounts of information at remarkable speed.

But it cannot jump into a swimming pool, taste a lobster, or personally check whether a hotel room really is quiet in the morning.

Someone has to explain reality to it.

## One Evening in Dubai, €300, and an Important Lesson

During my last trip to Dubai, a friend and I visited an extremely stylish Greek restaurant at the Atlantis complex.

Beautiful surroundings.

A spectacular view of the fountains.

Attentive service.

Everything looked fantastic.

Our dinner bill came to approximately €300. One lobster alone was around €180.

And the food?

Let's put it diplomatically: for that price, my taste buds were expecting a slightly more impressive fireworks display.

That's my personal experience, not a universal judgment about the restaurant's cuisine. Other guests may absolutely love it.

My friend still enjoyed the evening. She loved the atmosphere, the view, and the whole experience.

And me?

I love beautiful restaurants too. I appreciate excellent service, elegant surroundings, and spectacular views.

But when I go to a restaurant to enjoy good food **and** a beautiful atmosphere, I would quite like to have both, please.

Not just the view served on a silver platter.

But the important point is something else.

Next time, I'll organize the evening very differently.

First, we'll go somewhere we can genuinely enjoy a good meal. Perhaps a buffet offering a wide selection of dishes and delicious seafood.

Maybe I'll have lobster number one, lobster number two, and, just to be on the safe side, lobster number three.

Then I'll happily roll down the stairs, thoroughly satisfied, sit down with my friend in a lovely café or bar, and enjoy the fountains.

Perhaps with a cocktail.

Or, if the lobsters have won, with mineral water and lemon. 😅

**The same destination. Similar desired experiences. A completely different itinerary.**

And potentially a much better balance between what we spend and how much we actually enjoy ourselves.

Why does this matter?

Because we weren't simply looking for "the best restaurant."

We wanted to have a wonderful evening.

And that involved several wishes at the same time:

- Enjoy excellent food.
- Experience a beautiful atmosphere.
- Watch the fountains.
- Spend quality time together.
- Get something that genuinely matches our expectations and the money we're spending.

A single restaurant doesn't necessarily have to fulfill all these wishes perfectly.

Sometimes a better solution is a combination of several experiences.

**And that's exactly how I imagine an ideal holiday itinerary assembled by ChatGPT — or, in the future, by a personal AI agent: not merely a list of popular places, but a carefully combined experience that genuinely suits a particular person.**

## A Restaurant Can Be Excellent — and Still Be the Wrong Recommendation

Imagine two guests.

The first loves experimental cuisine. Small portions, unusual textures, surprising flavor combinations. The more creative, the better.

The second wants a proper piece of meat, potatoes, good bread, and preferably no artistically arranged foam that requires asking the waiter whether it's supposed to be eaten or admired as table decoration.

Both can have excellent taste.

Both can be perfectly willing to spend a lot of money.

But they need completely different recommendations.

And the description "fine dining" simply isn't enough.

Other details may be decisive:

How large are the portions?

Is the dish served hot, lukewarm, or cold?

What is its texture?

Does the food actually match the description on the menu?

And if the menu promises aubergines — are there actually any aubergines on the plate?

Sounds trivial?

Not to someone paying for the experience.

A Michelin-starred chef may be enthusiastic about a culinary concept. A restaurant critic may award it ten points.

And the guest can still say:

"Lovely for you. But I wanted something completely different for dinner."

**A professional evaluation doesn't automatically answer whether an offer matches the personal expectations of a particular individual.**

And exactly the same applies to hotels.

## A Different Question: What Is Your Hotel Actually Suitable For?

In February 2026, Seymone Leigh Moodley published an article in *Hotel & Catering News Middle East* about changes in luxury hospitality.

She described a development that I consider particularly important:

Travelers increasingly seek hotels that understand **why** they are traveling — whether for restoration, focus, personal connection, or long-term living.

The article is titled *The Trends Reshaping How We Stay, Live and Experience Hotels*:

https://hotelandcatering.com/news/the-trends-reshaping-how-we-stay-live-and-experience-hotels

I completely agree with this idea.

But I approach the same question from the opposite direction.

**I investigate which specific needs and travel situations a hotel is genuinely suitable for.**

A hotel doesn't have to be perfect for everyone.

It doesn't have to fulfill every possible wish.

And it certainly doesn't have to please every guest.

What matters is that its real strengths match the right people.

But now comes the question that drives my professional work:

**How is a traveler supposed to know before booking that this particular hotel genuinely meets their needs?**

And even more importantly:

How is ChatGPT supposed to recognize that match if the necessary information is missing?

## What Hotel Employees Know — and What Guests Actually Experience

During one hotel stay, I happened to notice stains on the ceiling.

Not because I was walking around the building with a flashlight.

Not because I was deliberately searching for defects.

I was simply standing in a particular spot. The light was hitting the ceiling at a particular angle.

And suddenly, the stains became visible.

I wouldn't have noticed them in the usual hotel photographs.

Probably not in an ordinary video either.

You had to be standing in exactly the right place, at exactly the right moment.

Another example is an unusual beach amenity, such as a menthol shower.

A website can tell you that such a shower exists.

But what does it actually feel like?

Is the sensation pleasant, intense, refreshing, or perhaps unsuitable for certain guests?

A marketing description can explain that a service is available.

It doesn't automatically communicate what experiencing that service feels like.

And this is where a problem arises that many hotels underestimate.

Their employees often know their own areas of responsibility very well.

Reception knows the room categories.

The restaurant knows the menu.

The technical department knows the equipment.

Marketing knows the selling points.

**But who understands the complete experience from the perspective of someone who has paid for their own holiday and now expects to receive something special in return?**

A guest doesn't experience departments.

A guest experiences a stay.

And sometimes a tiny detail determines whether someone leaves delighted or disappointed.

## The Sauna Exists. The Essential Information Is Still Missing.

While researching a hotel in Poland, I came across a revealing example.

The official website listed saunas among its facilities.

Wonderful.

However, an important detail was missing: the men's and women's sauna areas were separate.

For some guests, that information is completely irrelevant.

For others, it may determine whether they book the hotel at all.

A woman specifically looking for a separate women's wellness area might choose this hotel precisely because of that feature.

A couple hoping to enjoy the sauna together might interpret the very same information quite differently.

**One single characteristic can be an advantage for one guest and a limitation for another.**

The hotel already has this feature.

Nobody needs to build another wellness area.

Nobody needs to invent a new service.

The information simply needs to be complete, accurate, and understandable.

And this explicitly includes the hotel's own website.

If essential characteristics are missing there, we can hardly expect an external recommendation system to take them into account reliably.

Perhaps the person who created the website carefully checked every document.

Perhaps everything listed in the official facilities overview was included.

But a list of amenities doesn't automatically explain **why those amenities matter to a particular guest**.

## "But We Already Have Reviews!"

Yes, you do.

And reviews can be extremely useful.

However, there's a small problem.

Some guests write an entire novel after a bad experience.

When everything was wonderful, the review sometimes consists of just:

"Fantastic hotel! Great holiday! Would definitely return!"

That's lovely.

But what exactly was fantastic?

Why was this particular hotel right for that person?

Was it the tranquility?

The beach?

The separate sauna?

The breakfast?

The fact that the hotel was perfect for young children — or perhaps the fact that there weren't any children around?

All too often, these questions remain unanswered.

Reviews are also shaped by the individual expectations of the people writing them.

One traveler loves a lively atmosphere.

Another is looking for absolute silence.

One person considers a restaurant extraordinary, while another simply doesn't enjoy its cuisine.

That's why reviews are a valuable source of information.

**But they cannot replace a systematic investigation into which actual characteristics of a hotel are relevant to which travel needs.**

## This Is Where My Work Begins

I'm Anna Trocka, a marketing strategist and independent researcher based in Berlin.

With my own GEO methodology, which I developed specifically for this field, I don't simply investigate whether a hotel is visible in AI-generated responses.

What interests me most is this:

**Does artificial intelligence understand what this hotel actually offers — and can it determine for whom, and under which circumstances, the hotel is genuinely a suitable recommendation?**

That requires investigating the hotel's real characteristics.

Not merely what appears in its promotional materials.

Not just its popularity or ratings.

But the specific details that genuinely influence a particular traveler's decision.

The resulting information must then be verified, presented accurately, and made available within the digital information environment.

And this work may involve more than creating new content.

It may also require correcting or expanding information on the hotel's own website.

There is an important distinction here: publishing high-quality information does not automatically guarantee that a hotel will appear in ChatGPT recommendations.

The outcome depends on source accessibility, the freshness and reliability of the information, and how the particular AI system operates.

But without the necessary knowledge, even the most intelligent recommendation system may fail to recognize how well a property matches someone's needs.

## Content Is Not Just Text. And Content Is Not the Entire Information Environment.

There is a great deal of discussion about content in the GEO industry.

More articles.

More mentions.

More visibility.

Lovely.

But before talking about quantity, perhaps we should ask **which information is actually missing**.

Suppose a hotel has a particularly suitable wellness area for a specific type of traveler.

But its website describes that area only through generic promotional phrases.

This doesn't necessarily give an AI system a clear understanding of why the hotel is suitable for that particular guest.

Useful content must make such relationships understandable.

And the hotel's own website is only one part of its broader digital information environment.

There are also independent publications, reviews, photographs, videos, historical information, and sometimes outdated or contradictory claims.

This information environment needs to be investigated and, where necessary, corrected or supplemented.

Technical accessibility matters too.

And no: just because a piece of content exists somewhere online doesn't mean ChatGPT will necessarily find it, use it, or recommend the hotel.

I consider this distinction essential.

Google also emphasizes the value of original, helpful content based on genuine knowledge and firsthand experience in its guidance for generative search features.

Source: Google Search Central

https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide

Another article that merely rephrases the same ten marketing claims from a hotel's website doesn't necessarily contribute any new knowledge.

## A Hotel Doesn't Just Need Visibility. It Needs the Right Recommendation.

This is where my approach differs from GEO activities focused primarily on visibility.

One question is:

"How can we get ChatGPT to mention our hotel more often?"

My question is:

**"What does ChatGPT need to know about our hotel so that it recommends us precisely when we can genuinely fulfill the traveler's needs?"**

That may mean a hotel won't be recommended to every potential guest.

And that's perfectly fine.

What good is a booking from someone who arrives with completely unrealistic expectations?

Someone who wants a peaceful retreat but ends up at a lively party hotel?

Or someone who expects to enjoy the sauna with their partner, only to discover that the men's and women's areas are separate?

Such a guest may be dissatisfied even when the hotel delivers exactly what it promises.

**Not every hotel has to suit every guest. But the right guest should be able to understand why that particular hotel is right for them.**

This isn't just about customer satisfaction.

It also concerns the quality of future inquiries, expectations before booking, and ultimately the commercial opportunities available to a hotel.

Whether and to what extent this influences bookings and revenue needs to be evaluated in practice.

But the underlying issue is already apparent.

## From the Right Hotel to the Ideal Holiday Experience

Now let's take this one step further.

A traveler may not simply want to book a hotel room.

They may want to recover from a stressful period.

Enjoy excellent food.

Discover something new.

Spend quality time with family.

Or forget every responsibility in their life for a few days.

An intelligent travel agent can combine different offerings to create that experience.

Perhaps Hotel A is particularly suitable for accommodation.

Restaurant B serves exactly the kind of food the traveler loves.

And Bar C offers precisely the view they were hoping to enjoy that evening.

Why force everything into one single establishment when a combination of experiences might satisfy their wishes more effectively?

**The ideal holiday doesn't necessarily consist of one perfect place. It can be an individually assembled sequence of experiences that complement one another.**

ChatGPT can already help plan such experiences today.

Future AI agents may go further by taking additional steps and, where the necessary capabilities and integrations are available, preparing or completing reservations and bookings.

But the quality of such recommendations depends heavily on the reliable information available to those systems.

Which brings us right back to personal experience.

To familiarization trips.

To actually getting to know a product.

To information that isn't in the brochure.

## One Final Question for the Hotel Industry

Hotels invest enormous effort in selling their rooms.

They invest in distribution, marketing, booking platforms, travel operators, and customer loyalty.

And rightly so.

But as the way travelers obtain information and make decisions changes, another responsibility emerges:

**A hotel's genuine strengths must not only be understood by its marketing team and travel partners. They must also become understandable to AI-powered recommendation systems.**

This doesn't mean we need less human experience.

We need more of it — and a professional way to turn that experience into accessible, verifiable knowledge.

Artificial intelligence can compare, analyze, and plan.

It can combine different offerings into an itinerary suited to a particular person.

But it cannot personally discover what a lobster tastes like, whether a portion is worth its price, or how a menthol shower feels against your skin.

Human beings have to experience these things.

And someone needs to make sure the knowledge gained from those experiences isn't lost.

**For decades, hotels have invested in people who can recommend the right travel experiences to their guests.**

**The crucial question today is: who is making sure that ChatGPT and future AI agents understand your hotel well enough to recommend it to the right people?**

That's what my work is about.

And if, someday, a traveler enjoys a wonderful evening in Dubai thanks to a well-informed AI agent — happily full, with a considerably less depleted bank account, sitting by the fountains — then humans and machines will have accomplished something genuinely useful together.

Even if it took lobster number three to get there. 😅
